## How to use this manual

This manual describes how money moves through this firm and what the CFO is
responsible for at each step. It is written to be worked through in order the
first time and used as a reference afterwards.

Four conventions run through it:

**Procedures are numbered and meant to be followed literally.** Where a
procedure says to obtain something in writing, a verbal answer does not close
the step.

**Rules are described by subject, not cited by number** — except where a rule
number appears in a quoted judicial opinion, in which case it is given as the
court gave it. Rule numbering and text change. Cite the rule itself, not this
manual.

**Case notes are marked and carry a weight line.** A decision from another
state is persuasive, not binding, and this manual says which is which. Nothing
here is a substitute for an opinion from counsel admitted where the question
arises.

**Anything marked *Verify* must be confirmed before you rely on it.** Links are
landing pages and were not individually checked when this manual was issued.
Never quote a government fee, threshold, or processing time from this manual —
pull the live page.

### What this manual is not

It is not legal advice, and it does not resolve any question of professional
responsibility. Several sections identify questions that must be answered by
the firm's attorney or by outside ethics counsel. The CFO's role in those
sections is to produce the facts and escalate — not to decide.

## 1. Structure and authority

### 1.1 The single admission

Every dollar this firm bills traces back to one admission. José Rolando is
admitted in Puerto Rico. Everyone else at the firm works under that admission.

Nothing about that is unusual in immigration practice. It becomes consequential
in three places, and all three belong to the CFO: the boundary of what the
admission authorizes (1.2), what happens to revenue earned outside that
boundary (1.7), and the risk of the whole firm depending on one person (1.8).

### 1.2 The three authority buckets

Our work falls into three buckets. They are not the same, and the boundary does
not follow the office address.

| Bucket | Status | Basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Federal immigration** — USCIS, the immigration courts, the BIA | Authorized | Federal regulation defines an attorney as a member in good standing of the bar of any state, possession, territory, or commonwealth. Puerto Rico qualifies, and geography is irrelevant. [8 CFR Part 1001](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-V/subchapter-A/part-1001) · [8 CFR Part 292](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-V/subchapter-B/part-292) |
| **2. Federal district court** — mandamus and APA suits against USCIS | Admission required | Each district admits its own bar, and Texas districts commonly tie admission to Texas bar membership. Verify per district, every district. |
| **3. Texas state court** and advice on Texas law | Not covered | A Puerto Rico admission does not reach Texas state courts. Requires Texas admission, pro hac vice for a named case, or a Texas-licensed attorney genuinely of record. |

*Verify:* bucket 2 against the local rules of each federal district we file in
— [S.D. Tex.](https://www.txs.uscourts.gov/), [N.D. Tex.](https://www.txnd.uscourts.gov/),
[W.D. Tex.](https://www.txwd.uscourts.gov/), [E.D. Tex.](https://www.txed.uscourts.gov/)
— and bucket 3 with Texas counsel. Do not run the firm off this table.

### 1.3 Why bucket 1 holds: the preemption line

The rule that lets a Puerto Rico admittee practice immigration law in Texas is
not an immigration rule. It is a Supremacy Clause rule, and it is settled.

> **Case note — *Sperry v. Florida ex rel. Florida Bar*, 373 U.S. 379 (1963). Binding (U.S. Supreme Court).** Florida tried to enjoin a patent practitioner who was not a member of the Florida bar from preparing patent applications in Florida. The Court held it could not: where federal law authorizes a person to practice before a federal agency, "Florida may not deny to those failing to meet its own qualifications the right to perform the functions within the scope of the federal authority." A state may not enforce licensing requirements that give "the State's licensing board a virtual power of review over the federal determination" that a person is qualified.

> **Case note — *Surrick v. Killion*, 449 F.3d 520 (3d Cir. 2006). Persuasive; not binding in the Fifth Circuit.** Generalising *Sperry*: it "stands for the general proposition that where federal law authorizes an agent to practice before a federal tribunal, the federal law preempts a state's licensing requirements to the extent that those requirements hinder or obstruct the goals of federal law." The court allowed an attorney suspended from the state bar, but admitted to the federal district court, to maintain an office supporting that federal practice.

> **Case note — *In re Desilets*, 291 F.3d 925 (6th Cir. 2002). Persuasive; not binding in the Fifth Circuit.** Stated compactly: "When state licensing laws purport to prohibit lawyers from doing that which federal law entitles them to do, the state law must give way."

**What this means operationally.** Bucket 1 is not a grey area and should not
be treated as one internally. If someone at the firm — a bank, a landlord, a
new hire, a client — suggests the arrangement is irregular, the answer is that
federal immigration practice is authorized by federal regulation for any
member in good standing of any state or territorial bar, and the Supreme Court
settled the preemption question in 1963.

### 1.4 Why buckets 2 and 3 do not: the limit of the line

The same doctrine that protects bucket 1 defines where the protection stops.
*Sperry* preempts state licensing **to the extent of the federal
authorization** — no further. Work that is not federally authorized is governed
by ordinary state licensing law.

> **Case note — *Birbrower, Montalbano, Condon & Frank v. Superior Court*, 17 Cal. 4th 119, 949 P.2d 1 (1998). California; not binding in Texas. Read it anyway.** A New York firm performed legal work for a California client, in California, without California admission. The court held this was the unauthorized practice of law — and then reached the consequence that matters here: "To the extent the fee agreement allows payment for those illegal local services, it is void, and Birbrower is not entitled to recover fees under the agreement for those services." The agreement survived only as to the work lawfully performed in New York, and only because that portion could be severed.

Read *Birbrower* as a finance case. The sanction was not merely disciplinary.
The fee agreement was **void as to the unauthorized work**, and the firm could
not collect for it. Whether a Texas court would reach the same result is a
question for Texas counsel — but a CFO who assumes it would not is taking an
uncompensated risk with the receivable.

### 1.5 Procedure: the authority audit

Run this once in your first week, then quarterly.

1. Export every matter opened, worked, or closed in the last twelve months.
2. For each matter record: matter type, forum, the specific admission or
   authority relied on, revenue recognized in the period, and bucket.
3. Total revenue by bucket and express each as a percentage of the whole.
4. List separately every matter in bucket 2 or 3 with no named admission and
   no Texas attorney of record. Attach a dollar figure to that list.
5. Deliver the list to José Rolando and to outside ethics counsel the same
   week. Do not hold it for a monthly report.
6. Record the date delivered and the response received. File both.

**Output:** a revenue split across three buckets, and a named exceptions list
with a number against it.

**Boundary:** you produce the number and escalate it. The attorney and ethics
counsel decide what it means. Do not opine on whether a matter was properly
handled, and do not re-characterize a matter to make it fit a bucket.

### 1.6 Procedure: classifying a new matter

Applies at intake, before the engagement letter is signed.

1. Identify the forum the matter will be filed or heard in.
2. Identify the authority that permits this firm to appear there.
3. If the answer is not bucket 1, do not open the matter until either a named
   admission or a Texas attorney of record is recorded against it.
4. Record the bucket on the matter record. It is a reportable field, not a note.
5. If the matter cannot be classified, that is an escalation, not a judgment
   call. See 1.9.

### 1.7 Authority as a financial-statement issue

This is the section to reread before signing off on any set of accounts.

Revenue from work the firm was not authorized to perform may not be
collectible, and fees already collected for such work may be refundable. That
makes authority classification an input to three ordinary accounting
judgements:

| Judgement | Why authority matters |
| --- | --- |
| **Revenue recognition** | Collectibility is a precondition to recognizing revenue. If a fee agreement may be void as to certain work, the consideration for that work is not probable of collection |
| **Receivable valuation** | An unauthorized-work receivable may be uncollectible as a matter of law, not merely of credit |
| **Contingent liabilities** | Fees already collected for unauthorized work may be refundable, and refund exposure is a liability, not a footnote |

**Do not book a provision, restate anything, or write anything down on your own
initiative.** Produce the analysis, escalate it under 1.9, and act on written
advice. A unilateral accounting adjustment in this area creates its own
problems.

### 1.8 Concentration risk

One admission carries one hundred percent of the firm's billable authority. If
it stops — illness, suspension, a disciplinary complaint, retirement — the firm
cannot lawfully bill for work in progress. Not reduced. Stopped.

Treat this the way you would treat a single customer who is the entire order
book. The CFO owns the mitigations:

| Mitigation | What "done" looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Succession plan | Written, naming who takes the files and on what authority, approved before it is needed |
| Bench strength | A second admitted attorney, or standing Texas co-counsel under a signed retainer |
| Key-person cover | Disability and key-person cover sized to revenue actually at stake, reviewed annually |
| Malpractice cover | A policy that accurately describes the jurisdictions we practice in. Misdescribing scope is how firms discover an exclusion during a claim |
| Cash buffer | Enough operating reserve to wind down or transfer matters in an orderly way |
| File continuity | Client files, passwords, and matter status accessible to someone other than the attorney |

### 1.9 Escalation

| Trigger | Goes to | Timeframe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A matter cannot be classified into a bucket | Attorney, then ethics counsel | Same day |
| Bucket 2 or 3 revenue with no cover | Attorney and ethics counsel | Same week, in writing |
| Any trust account discrepancy | Attorney | Immediately, before any further transfer |
| A disciplinary inquiry from any jurisdiction | Attorney and insurer | Same day |
| Suspected uncollectibility arising from authority | Attorney, then auditor or CPA | Before the next close |
| A client asks who is representing them | Attorney | Before answering |

## 2. Money held for clients

This is the chapter where an honest mistake ends careers. Read it before you
touch the account.

### 2.1 Which rules govern us

Two bodies of professional conduct rules can reach this firm at once.

| Jurisdiction | Why it reaches us |
| --- | --- |
| **Puerto Rico** | Where the admission lives. The Canons of Professional Ethics adopted by the Supreme Court of Puerto Rico govern the attorney, and the PR disciplinary authority can act on conduct anywhere. [Rama Judicial de Puerto Rico](https://poderjudicial.pr/) · [Colegio de Abogados y Notarios de PR](https://www.capr.org/) |
| **Texas** | Where the practice, the clients, the office, and the bank account are. Texas asserts disciplinary authority over lawyers who practice here whether or not they are admitted here. [Texas Center for Legal Ethics](https://www.legalethicstexas.com/) |

**The working rule until counsel says otherwise:** comply with whichever
requirement is stricter on every point, hold client funds in Texas at an
approved institution, and obtain a written choice-of-law opinion.

*Verify:* this is a genuine legal question, not an accounting preference. Get
the opinion in writing, keep it on file, and re-request it if the firm's
footprint changes. A documented decision you can produce on request is worth
more than being right by accident.

Texas IOLTA is administered by the
[Texas Access to Justice Foundation](https://www.teajf.org/). Confirm our trust
account is actually at an approved institution. Do not assume the bank handled
it when the account was opened.

### 2.2 The absolute rules

These do not bend for cash flow, for a good quarter, or for a client who said
it was fine.

- Client money is not the firm's money.
- Unearned fees stay in trust until earned.
- Firm money does not sit in trust, beyond any minimum the bank requires to
  keep the account open.
- No transfer out of trust without a documented earning event.
- No disbursement against uncollected funds.
- Trust is never overdrawn. There is no such thing as a small trust overdraft.
- Every client's balance is tracked individually, not just in aggregate.

Under a single-admission structure, a trust violation is not a fine and a
lesson. It is an extinction event for the firm.

### 2.3 Procedure: receiving client funds

1. Determine whether the funds are earned on receipt. In a flat-fee immigration
   practice they usually are not — see 3.2.
2. Deposit unearned funds into the trust account, never into operating.
3. Record the deposit against the individual client ledger the same day.
4. Where the payment covers a government filing fee, record that portion as
   funds held for a specific purpose, not as firm revenue. See 5.2.
5. If a card processor nets its fee out of the deposit, stop. A processor that
   deducts fees from a trust deposit creates an ethics problem. Use legal-
   specific processing that debits fees from operating.

### 2.4 Procedure: earning a flat fee

1. Identify the earning milestones in the signed fee agreement. If you cannot
   find them, escalate — see 3.4.
2. Confirm the milestone has actually occurred, from the case management
   system, not from a conversation.
3. Prepare a transfer for the amount earned at that milestone, and no more.
4. Record the earning event against the client ledger with the date, the
   milestone, and the evidence relied on.
5. Transfer trust to operating. Never the reverse.
6. Recognize revenue in the period the milestone occurred. See chapter 6.

### 2.5 Procedure: monthly three-way reconciliation

Run this every month, without exception, and do the first one by hand.

1. Reconcile the trust bank statement to the trust general ledger.
2. Reconcile the trust general ledger to the sum of all individual client
   ledgers.
3. Confirm all three agree to the cent. They must agree, not approximately
   agree.
4. Investigate any difference before doing anything else. Do not net, do not
   carry forward, do not adjust to make it balance.
5. Confirm no individual client ledger has a negative balance. A negative
   client balance means one client's funds were used for another's matter.
6. Have the reconciliation reviewed and signed by the attorney.
7. File it.

### 2.6 Records to keep

At minimum, and for the longer of the two jurisdictions' retention periods:

- Bank statements, cancelled items, and deposit records for the trust account
- A general ledger for the trust account
- An individual ledger for every client whose funds we hold
- Every signed monthly three-way reconciliation
- Records of every trust-to-operating transfer and the earning event behind it
- Signed fee agreements
- Records of funds refunded to clients

*Verify:* retention periods differ between jurisdictions. Confirm which applies
and apply the longer one.

### 2.7 Red flags

Any of these means stop and escalate the same day:

- The three-way reconciliation does not tie.
- A client ledger is negative.
- A transfer out of trust has no documented earning event behind it.
- Trust funds were used to cover an operating shortfall, even briefly, even
  repaid.
- A filing fee was collected but the filing was never made.
- Someone asks you to delay a reconciliation.

## 3. Engagement and fees

The fee agreement is the document that decides when the firm's money becomes
the firm's money. It is a finance document as much as a legal one.

### 3.1 What the fee agreement must do

For the CFO's purposes, an agreement is workable when it answers all of these
without interpretation:

- What the scope of the engagement is, and what falls outside it.
- What the fee is, and what it covers.
- Which costs are passed through, and how filing fees are handled.
- **At what defined milestones portions of the fee are earned.**
- What happens on early termination, by either side.
- How and when the client is billed for anything outside the flat fee.

If the earning milestones are absent or vague, the firm cannot evidence when a
transfer out of trust was proper. That is a compliance defect and an accounting
defect at the same time.

### 3.2 True retainer versus advance fee

These are different things and only one of them is the firm's money on receipt.

> **Case note — *Cluck v. Commission for Lawyer Discipline*, 214 S.W.3d 736 (Tex. App.—Austin 2007). Texas intermediate appellate authority.** An attorney took a "non-refundable retainer" of $15,000, billed his hourly rate against it, and deposited it in his operating account. The court held that was a violation: the payment was not a true retainer but an advance fee, which "belongs to the client until the services are rendered and must be held in a trust account." The court quoted the Texas ethics opinion on the point directly: **"A fee is not earned simply because it is designated as non-refundable."** A true retainer — one that genuinely compensates the lawyer for availability and lost opportunity — may be treated differently; a prepayment for services may not.

> **Case note — *In re Sather*, 3 P.3d 403 (Colo. 2000). Colorado; persuasive only.** The clearest statement of the general rule: "all client funds — including engagement retainers, advance fees, flat fees, lump sum fees, etc. — must be held in trust until there is a basis on which to conclude that the attorney 'earned' the fee; otherwise, the funds must remain in the client's trust account because they are not the attorney's property." The attorney there had spent a $20,000 flat fee on receipt, believing that was common practice. It was not a defence.

**The practical test**, drawn from *Cluck*: if the agreement bills work against
the payment, it is a prepayment for services, not a retainer for availability —
whatever the document calls it. Almost every immigration flat fee is a
prepayment.

### 3.3 "Non-refundable" is not a magic word

Labelling a fee non-refundable does not make it earned, does not make it the
firm's property, and does not permit depositing it into operating. If our
current agreements rely on that label, that is a finding for the attorney.

### 3.4 Procedure: reviewing a fee agreement as CFO

You are not approving its legal content. You are confirming it is operable.

1. Locate the earning milestones. If you cannot find them in under a minute,
   neither can a client.
2. Confirm each milestone is objectively verifiable from the case management
   system — a filing made, a receipt notice issued, an interview attended.
3. Confirm the amount earned at each milestone is stated, not implied.
4. Confirm filing fees are described as pass-through, and are not swept into
   the flat fee in a way that makes the pass-through untraceable.
5. Confirm there is a stated basis for refunding unearned fees on early
   termination.
6. Where any of the above fails, write it up and give it to the attorney. Do
   not redraft the agreement yourself.

### 3.5 Early termination and refunds

A client may discharge the firm at any time. On discharge, unearned fees must
be returned promptly — delay is itself a disciplinary problem, independent of
whether the money was eventually repaid. Keep enough in trust at all times to
make that possible without reference to the operating account.

## 4. The product: immigration filings

Bucket one is most of our book. A case there is a sequence of federal filings,
each carrying a fee, a clock, and a billing trigger.

### 4.1 What a case is

- **A fee.** The government's cut is a pass-through cost, not revenue.
- **A clock.** Processing time decides when the next milestone can be billed
  and when cash actually lands. It is the firm's working-capital cycle.
- **A trigger.** Each milestone is the moment a slice of the flat fee stops
  being the client's money and becomes ours.

### 4.2 Cohort risk: the Visa Bulletin

When a category in the [Visa Bulletin](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/legal/visa-law0/visa-bulletin.html)
retrogresses, every case behind that priority date stalls — and so does the
revenue attached to it. This is a revenue risk the firm does not control.

1. Track the active caseload by category and priority date.
2. Model what a retrogression in each significant category does to the next two
   quarters of milestone billing.
3. Review at each monthly close.

## 5. Revenue recognition

### 5.1 Two different questions

These are constantly confused, and confusing them is how firms get into
trouble. They have different answers, different authorities, and both must be
satisfied.

| Question | Governed by | Answer for a flat fee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Whose money is it?** | The professional conduct rules | The client's, until earned. Held in trust |
| **When is it revenue?** | The accounting framework | As performance obligations are satisfied |

Satisfying one does not satisfy the other. Money can be lawfully held in trust
and still be wrongly recognized as revenue, and vice versa.

### 5.2 Filing fees are never revenue

A government filing fee collected from a client is not revenue and never was.
Booking it as revenue inflates the top line and hides the real margin.

1. Collect the filing fee as funds held for a specific purpose.
2. Hold it in trust unless and until it is paid to the government.
3. Pay it and record the disbursement against the client ledger.
4. Never recognize it as revenue at any point in that sequence.
5. Reconcile collected-but-unpaid filing fees monthly. A fee collected and not
   filed is both a client problem and a trust problem.

### 5.3 Procedure: recognizing flat-fee revenue

1. Identify the performance obligations in the engagement — normally the same
   milestones the fee agreement uses for earning.
2. Allocate the fee across those obligations on a defensible basis, and
   document the basis once rather than re-deriving it each period.
3. Recognize each portion when that obligation is satisfied.
4. Reconcile recognized revenue against trust-to-operating transfers for the
   period. They should track closely; a persistent gap means the earning
   milestones and the performance obligations have drifted apart.
5. Where collectibility is in doubt for any reason — including authority, see
   1.7 — do not recognize. Escalate.

### 5.4 Deferred revenue and the trust balance

The trust balance and the deferred revenue balance answer related but distinct
questions, and both belong on the CFO's monthly review. A trust balance that
never moves while matters progress suggests earning events are not being
processed. A deferred revenue balance materially below the trust balance
suggests revenue is being recognized ahead of earning.

## 6. Pricing and profitability

### 6.1 The five metrics

| Metric | Formula | Question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Utilization | billable hours ÷ available hours | Are we busy? |
| Realization | amount billed ÷ amount worked | What did we give away? |
| Collection | amount collected ÷ amount billed | What did we never get paid? |
| Client acquisition cost | marketing spend ÷ new signed matters | What does a client cost to win? |
| **Effective hourly rate** | **flat fee ÷ hours actually spent** | **Which work is actually worth doing?** |

Benchmarks for the first four:
[Clio Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) — free,
annual, best available for a firm our size. Broader context:
[Thomson Reuters Institute](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/institute) ·
[Georgetown Center on Ethics and the Legal Profession](https://www.law.georgetown.edu/legal-profession-center/).

### 6.2 Procedure: effective hourly rate by case type

Run quarterly.

1. Select a case type from the matter classification.
2. Pull every matter of that type closed in the last twelve months.
3. Sum all recorded hours against those matters, from every timekeeper.
4. Sum the flat fees actually earned, excluding pass-through filing fees.
5. Divide fees by hours.
6. Compare against our standard hourly rate and against other case types.
7. Weight by authority bucket. A strong effective hourly rate in bucket 3 is an
   exposure, not a growth opportunity.

**Expect the hours to be incomplete.** Flat-fee matters are chronically
under-recorded because nobody bills against them. If time capture on flat-fee
work is poor, fixing it is a prerequisite to trusting this metric.

### 6.3 Procedure: quarterly case-mix review

1. Refresh the effective hourly rate for every case type.
2. Refresh the authority audit (1.5).
3. Identify one case type to do more of and one to stop taking.
4. Present both to the attorney with the arithmetic behind them.
5. Record the decision, including a decision to change nothing.

With one attorney carrying every matter, those hours are the scarcest thing
this firm owns. Choosing the wrong case mix is a capacity problem before it is
a margin problem.

## 7. Billing, collections, and receivables

### 7.1 What is collectible

Three separate reasons a fee may not be collectible, and only the first is
ordinary credit risk:

1. **The client cannot pay.** Ordinary provisioning.
2. **The fee was never earned.** Not a receivable at all — it is client money
   sitting in trust, or it is nothing.
3. **The engagement may not have been authorized.** See 1.7 and the *Birbrower*
   case note at 1.4. This is a legal defect in the right to the fee.

Keep the three separate in the ledger. Netting them produces an aged
receivable that cannot be acted on.

### 7.2 Procedure: monthly receivable review

1. Age the receivable by matter and by case type.
2. Separate genuinely billed-and-unpaid amounts from unearned amounts sitting
   in trust. They are not the same thing and must never be reported together.
3. Flag any receivable arising from a bucket 2 or 3 matter for the escalation
   in 1.9.
4. Identify matters where work continues while nothing has been collected.
5. Report the collection rate against the trailing twelve months.

### 7.3 Collections in an immigration practice

Two constraints shape collections here and both are real:

- Withdrawing from a matter for non-payment is constrained by the attorney's
  professional obligations, and in a matter before a court or agency it may
  require leave. Non-payment is not by itself a licence to stop work. Route
  every proposed withdrawal through the attorney.
- Clients in immigration matters are often making significant sacrifices to
  pay. Structured payment plans, agreed in writing at intake and tracked, are
  ordinarily better for both sides than aggressive collection later.

### 8.3 Open question: Puerto Rico

An admission in Puerto Rico is not by itself a tax nexus. An entity, an office,
or resident work there would change the picture.

1. Put the question to the firm's CPA in writing in your first week.
2. Obtain the answer in writing.
3. File it with the choice-of-law opinion from 2.1.
4. Re-ask if the firm's footprint changes.

### 8.4 Employment traps

**Paralegal classification.** Exempt versus non-exempt is the most common and
most expensive payroll mistake law firms make. Review how our paralegals are
classified and document the basis.

## 9. Systems and controls

### 9.1 The system-of-record map

Document which system is authoritative for each of the following. Where two
systems both claim one, that is a finding.

- Matters, matter status, and authority bucket
- Time recorded
- Invoices issued
- Cash received and held
- Client trust balances

Immigration case management: [Docketwise](https://www.docketwise.com/) ·
[INSZoom](https://www.inszoom.com/) · [Cerenade](https://www.cerenade.com/) ·
[LollyLaw](https://www.lollylaw.com/)

Practice management: [Clio](https://www.clio.com/) · [MyCase](https://www.mycase.com/)

Payments and accounting: [LawPay](https://www.lawpay.com/) ·
[QuickBooks Online](https://quickbooks.intuit.com/), with a chart of accounts
that separates trust from operating.

Every place a person retypes a number from one system into another is a place
our numbers will eventually disagree. Enumerate those points; that list is the
first process backlog.

### 9.2 Procedure: monthly close

1. Complete the three-way trust reconciliation (2.5) first. Nothing else in the
   close is meaningful if trust does not tie.
2. Reconcile the operating account.
3. Confirm every trust-to-operating transfer has a documented earning event.
4. Reconcile collected-but-unpaid filing fees.
5. Recognize revenue by performance obligation, not by cash receipt (5.3).
6. Review deferred revenue against the trust balance (5.4).
7. Run the receivable review (7.2).
8. Update the rolling 13-week cash flow forecast.
9. Update the metrics dashboard.
10. Close the period and file the pack, including the signed reconciliation.

### 9.3 Minimum internal controls

For a firm this size, segregation of duties is imperfect by necessity. These
are the minimum compensating controls:

- The person who reconciles the trust account is not the only person who can
  move money out of it.
- Every trust disbursement has a second pair of eyes before release.
- The attorney reviews and signs the monthly trust reconciliation.
- Bank statements are received by someone other than the person who reconciles.
- No shared logins for banking or payment processing.
- Authority bucket is a required field at matter opening and cannot be left
  blank.

## Appendix C. Table of authorities

Every case below was retrieved and its quoted language verified against the
opinion text. **Weight is stated because it matters:** only *Sperry* binds
everyone. The rest are persuasive here to differing degrees, and none of them
substitutes for advice from counsel admitted where the question arises. Case
law also moves — confirm each remains good law before relying on it.

| Authority | Weight here | Proposition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Sperry v. Florida ex rel. Florida Bar*, 373 U.S. 379 (1963) | Binding — U.S. Supreme Court | Where federal law authorizes practice before a federal agency, state licensing requirements are preempted to that extent. A state may not exercise "a virtual power of review over the federal determination." The foundation of bucket 1 |
| *Surrick v. Killion*, 449 F.3d 520 (3d Cir. 2006) | Persuasive — outside our circuit | Generalizes *Sperry*: federal authorization to practice before a federal tribunal preempts state licensing to the extent state rules "hinder or obstruct the goals of federal law" |
| *In re Desilets*, 291 F.3d 925 (6th Cir. 2002) | Persuasive — outside our circuit | "When state licensing laws purport to prohibit lawyers from doing that which federal law entitles them to do, the state law must give way" |
| *Birbrower, Montalbano, Condon & Frank v. Superior Court*, 17 Cal. 4th 119, 949 P.2d 1 (1998) | Not binding in Texas — read for the consequence | Out-of-state practice not federally authorized is UPL, and a fee agreement is void as to that work: the firm "is not entitled to recover fees under the agreement for those services," severable only as to lawfully performed work |
| *Cluck v. Commission for Lawyer Discipline*, 214 S.W.3d 736 (Tex. App.—Austin 2007) | Texas intermediate appellate | "A fee is not earned simply because it is designated as non-refundable." An advance fee belongs to the client until services are rendered and must be held in trust; a payment billed against by the hour is not a true retainer |
| *In re Sather*, 3 P.3d 403 (Colo. 2000) | Persuasive only — Colorado | All client funds, "including engagement retainers, advance fees, flat fees, lump sum fees," must be held in trust until earned, because until then they "are not the attorney's property" |

## Appendix E. Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Advance fee** | A prepayment for services not yet rendered. Client property until earned |
| **Authority bucket** | This firm's classification of a matter by what permits us to handle it. See 1.2 |
| **BIA** | Board of Immigration Appeals |
| **Earning event** | The documented milestone at which part of a flat fee stops being client money and becomes firm revenue |

| **IOLTA** | Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts |
