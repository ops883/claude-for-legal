# Venezuela — Country Conditions Packet

**Prepared:** July 27, 2026
**Scope:** Asylum (INA § 208), Withholding of Removal (INA § 241(b)(3)), and
protection under the Convention Against Torture (8 C.F.R. §§ 1208.16–.18)
**Risk profile centered:** Political opposition members, activists, campaign
workers, and perceived regime opponents
**Currency of research:** Sources located through July 27, 2026

## 1. Executive summary — the central problem in your case

The dispositive country-conditions issue for a Venezuelan political-opinion
claim in the second half of 2026 is **no longer whether the Maduro government
persecuted opponents.** That is well documented and largely uncontested. The
issue is whether the events of January–February 2026 — the U.S. capture of
Maduro, the installation of an interim government, the mass release of political
prisoners, and the February 2026 Amnesty Law — constitute a **fundamental change
in circumstances** that rebuts the presumption of future persecution under
8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i)(A).

Expect DHS to argue exactly that. Expect it in every Venezuelan case, including
cases where past persecution is conceded.

**The rebuttal, in one line:** the actors changed at the top; the persecutory
apparatus did not. The UN Fact-Finding Mission — the single most authoritative
source on this question — found in March 2026 that the structures sustaining
political persecution "have not been dismantled," that the legal instruments
underpinning persecution "remain fully in force," and that it had already
documented **at least 87 new politically motivated detentions** in the ten weeks
*after* Maduro's capture. Independent monitors count **hundreds** of people still
arbitrarily detained on political grounds as of mid-2026. Chavismo remains in
power under interim President Delcy Rodríguez — Maduro's own Vice President.

Four evidentiary pillars support that rebuttal, developed in §§ 4–7 below:

| Pillar | Core proposition | Lead source |
|---|---|---|
| **Continuity of the apparatus** | SEBIN, DGCIM, GNB command structures unreformed; persecution laws still in force | UN FFM, March 12, 2026 oral update |
| **New detentions after the transition** | ≥ 87 politically motivated detentions since Jan. 3, 2026 | UN FFM, March 12, 2026 |
| **The amnesty is incomplete and defective** | Excludes many arbitrarily detained; applied to deny eligible people; convictions stand | HRW, May 13, 2026 |
| **Hundreds remain detained** | 389–485+ political detainees mid-2026, depending on methodology | Foro Penal; Amnesty International |

A change in the identity of the head of state is not, standing alone, a
fundamental change in circumstances where the institutions that carried out the
persecution remain intact and operational. That is the argument this packet is
built to support.

## 3. Political prisoner counts — how to handle the numbers

Counts diverge because organizations define the population differently. Do not
treat the divergence as a weakness; explain it.

| Date | Count | Source | Definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| End 2025 | 902 | Foro Penal (as cited in Freedom House FIW 2026) | Political prisoners at year's end |
| Feb. 24, 2026 | 573+ | Foro Penal | Still detained; 436 verified releases since Jan. 8 |
| Mar. 2, 2026 | 526 | Foro Penal | Detained on political grounds |
| Apr. 16, 2026 | 485+ | Amnesty International (AMR 53/0884/2026) | **Arbitrarily** detained for political reasons |
| June 9, 2026 | 389 | Foro Penal | 357 men / 32 women; 166 convicted, 223 unconvicted |
| Since Jan. 2026 | 670 released | Foro Penal | Cumulative verified releases |

**Two points to make with this table.**

*First*, the trend line is real but the floor is not zero — and it is not
approaching zero. Several hundred people remain held on political grounds
eighteen months after the election that triggered the crackdown and six months
after the transition. A regime that has released two-thirds of its political
prisoners while continuing to hold hundreds more, and while arresting new ones,
has not stopped persecuting people; it has adjusted the volume.

*Second*, government figures and independently verified figures are far apart.
The National Assembly claimed **more than 3,200** people "fully released" under
the amnesty by late February 2026 (3,052 of whom were already at liberty under
house arrest or other restrictive measures, and only **179** actually released
from prison). Foro Penal verified **91** political prisoners freed in the same
window following the law's passage. Where the government's own accounting
inflates its numbers by an order of magnitude, its representations about the
completeness of the amnesty are entitled to no weight.

## 4. Pillar one — the persecutory apparatus survived the transition

This is the most important section of the packet.

**UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Venezuela, oral update to
the Human Rights Council (61st session), March 12, 2026** — the Mission found
that Venezuela's repressive state machinery **remained operational** after
Maduro's apprehension. As reported:

- the structures that have sustained persecution for years **have not been
  dismantled**, and no State policy has been announced to begin dismantling
  them;
- **"the legal instruments that have long served as a basis for political
  persecution remain fully in force"**;
- **"State institutions that played a key role in the repression have not been
  reviewed or reformed."**

Expert members told the Council the machinery of repression was **"mutating"**
under the interim government rather than being dismantled.

**Command continuity in the implicated agencies.** Reporting through 2026
indicates that command hierarchies in **SEBIN** (Bolivarian National Intelligence
Service) and **DGCIM** (General Directorate of Military Counterintelligence) —
the two agencies most associated with torture and enforced disappearance —
**remain unaltered**. The announced closure and repurposing of **El Helicoide**,
SEBIN's Caracas headquarters and the site most frequently used to hold victims
of enforced disappearance, is a change of venue, not of institution: SEBIN
continued to operate as a detention and transport agency after the announcement
(see § 10 on the June 2026 deportee deaths, where SEBIN agents transported and
confined returnees).

**Bolivarian National Guard — crimes against humanity.** In its **December 2025**
report, the FFM found that GNB officials committed serious violations and
**crimes against humanity** over more than a decade: arbitrary deprivation of
life, arbitrary detention, sexual and gender-based violence, and torture, in
policing of protests and in **targeted political persecution** since 2014. The
Mission found the GNB was **a central actor in the crime against humanity of
persecution on political grounds**. The GNB was not disbanded or restructured in
2026.

**Continuing FFM mandate and findings on Maduro.** The HRC extended the
Mission's mandate through October 2026. The Mission has stated it has reasonable
grounds to believe Maduro is responsible for crimes against humanity against the
civilian population.

> **Framing for the brief:** persecution under the Refugee Convention is
> institutional, not personal. Where the agencies, the legal instruments, the
> judiciary, and the officer corps that carried out a decade of political
> persecution all remain in place, the removal of one official — by a foreign
> military, not by domestic political change — does not establish that the
> applicant's fear is no longer well-founded.

## 5. Pillar two — new politically motivated detentions after the transition

The FFM's March 12, 2026 update documented, **after** Maduro's January 3
capture:

- **at least 87 new politically motivated detentions**;
- **14 journalists and media workers** detained while covering Rodríguez's
  swearing-in on January 5, 2026;
- **at least 27 people detained for allegedly celebrating Maduro's detention —
  including 15 children.**

For the immediately preceding period (**September–December 2025**) the Mission
documented **135 arbitrary arrests** and a continued pattern of **torture and
ill-treatment** in prisons, detention centers, and clandestine **"secure
houses."**

Amnesty International reported that as of mid-2026, hundreds of politically
motivated arbitrary detainees remained imprisoned and that it **continued to
receive reports of torture and possible enforced disappearance** of detainees
under the interim government.

That people were detained for *celebrating the fall of the prior head of state*
is the single most useful fact in this packet. It demonstrates that the conduct
being punished is still the expression of political opinion, that the punishment
survived the change of government, and that the class of people at risk has
*expanded* rather than contracted.

## 6. Pillar three — the Amnesty Law is incomplete, defective, and revocable

### The law itself — official citation

**Ley de Amnistía para la Convivencia Democrática** ("Amnesty Law for
Democratic Coexistence"), published in **Gaceta Oficial Extraordinaria
No. 6.990, February 19, 2026**; promulgated by Delcy Eloína Rodríguez Gómez as
interim president; **16 articles** plus a final provision; **entered into force
on publication**. Article 1 states its purpose as granting a general and full
amnesty for offenses within the facts and temporal scope defined in the law, to
promote "social peace and democratic coexistence."

The official text is available from the National Assembly:
`https://www.asambleanacional.gob.ve/storage/documentos/botones/ley-de-amnistia-2026-20260223000136.pdf`
(see also Acceso a la Justicia's annotated version, source #45).

**Statutory exclusions (as enacted).** The law excludes from amnesty:

- grave violations of human rights, **crimes against humanity, and war crimes**;
- **intentional homicide** and grievous bodily harm;
- **drug trafficking** and psychotropic substances offenses;
- offenses under the **anti-corruption law**.

Note the asymmetry, and make it explicit in the brief: the exclusions bar relief
for *the perpetrators* of state crimes — which is defensible as transitional
justice — but the categories are drafted broadly enough that the same
prosecutors and judges who fabricated "terrorism," "conspiracy," and "incitement
to hatred" charges against protesters retain discretion to characterize a
detainee's conduct as falling outside the amnesty. That is precisely what HRW
found happening in practice.

### How it is being applied

**Human Rights Watch, "Venezuela: Exclusions, Procedures Mar Amnesty Law,"
May 13, 2026.** HRW concluded the law has **serious shortcomings**: it excludes
many people who were arbitrarily detained, and it is being **applied in ways that
deny release to people who should be eligible**. Specifically:

- **Exclusions.** Many opposition members, journalists, and human rights
  defenders who had been arbitrarily detained were excluded from relief.
- **Broadly defined carve-outs.** The law contains vaguely drafted provisions
  **allowing judges to exclude people detained for acts amounting to protected
  speech** — i.e., the exclusions track precisely the conduct that grounds a
  political-opinion asylum claim.
- **Procedural defects.** Applicants were not properly informed of the charges
  or evidence against them; judges failed to justify or explain denials;
  authorities restricted access to case files and to **privately retained
  counsel**.
- **Convictions stand.** Although the law requires deletion of criminal and
  administrative records and cancellation of related arrest and location
  requests, **convictions remain in place in some cases**, leaving beneficiaries
  exposed to **re-arrest**.

**Structural limits.** UN experts warned in February 2026 that the law must apply to
all unlawfully prosecuted victims and be grounded in transitional justice; the
FFM "cautiously welcomed" the draft while urging transparency and public
participation. Neither condition was satisfied in the final text or its
administration.

**Why this matters legally.** An amnesty administered by the same judiciary that
imposed the underlying convictions, that leaves convictions intact, that
preserves discretion to exclude people for protected speech, and that denies
applicants counsel of choice, is not a durable grant of protection. It is
revocable at the discretion of the persecutor. A person released under it has
not received state protection; they have received a suspended threat.

**Watch this issue:** no USCIS or EOIR guidance treating the Venezuelan amnesty
as a changed-conditions development had issued as of July 27, 2026 — this was
searched for specifically and nothing was found. Practitioner commentary
anticipates such guidance. Re-check before any individual hearing; if a
memorandum does issue, it is agency guidance and not binding on the IJ's
country-conditions findings, which must rest on the record evidence.

## 7. Pillar four — torture and enforced disappearance (CAT)

**Amnesty International, "The Crime of Enforced Disappearance in Venezuela:
Detentions Without a Trace," AMR 53/0083/2025 (July 2025)** — the anchor CAT
document. Amnesty concluded that **enforced disappearances in Venezuela amount
to crimes against humanity**. Findings include:

- **El Helicoide**, SEBIN headquarters, is among the sites most frequently used
  to hold victims of enforced disappearance;
- evidence that disappeared persons were **subjected to torture or other
  ill-treatment to extract or fabricate "confessions"**;
- the report covers the period beginning with the **July 28, 2024** presidential
  election and the repression that followed.

**Documented methods** (across Amnesty and FFM reporting on the post-2024
crackdown): beatings; **electric shocks**; **asphyxiation with plastic bags**;
**sexual torture**; prolonged **solitary confinement**; confinement in tiny,
dark, overcrowded punishment cells; **incommunicado detention** — in some cases
from the day of arrest — with denial of visits, phone calls, and access to
privately retained counsel.

**Continuing into 2026.** Amnesty's April 16, 2026 urgent action
(**AMR 53/0884/2026**, "Venezuela: Hundreds still arbitrarily detained," the
sixth in the 72/24 series) documented at least **485** still arbitrarily
detained, with applications rejected or statuses altered notwithstanding the
amnesty. Named detainees include **Emirlendris Benítez, María Auxiliadora
Delgado, Juan Carlos Marrufo, Danner Barajas, and Damián Rojas**; **Perkins
Rocha** and **Rafael Ramírez** remained under house arrest. A further urgent
action, **AMR 53/1291/2026** ("Amidst disaster, hundreds remain detained," the
eighth in the series), was issued after the June earthquakes.

**For the CAT analysis**, these sources establish the two required elements
independently of the applicant's own testimony: (1) torture of politically
detained persons is **systematic and ongoing**, not historical; and (2) it is
inflicted **by or with the acquiescence of public officials** — indeed by named
state security agencies whose command structures are unchanged. Note that CAT
protection does not require nexus to a protected ground and is **mandatory** if
the standard is met, which makes it the more durable form of relief where a
changed-circumstances argument threatens the asylum claim.

## 8. Opposition figures and activists — the targeted class

- **Arrest warrants remain outstanding** for opposition leadership, including
  **Edmundo González Urrutia** (the widely recognized winner of the July 28,
  2024 election) and **María Corina Machado** (2025 Nobel Peace Prize laureate),
  forcing many opposition figures into hiding or exile.
- Machado spent roughly **15 months underground** facing death threats, and was
  **briefly detained** when security forces intercepted her convoy leaving a
  Caracas rally — her motorcycle forced off the road amid gunfire.
- The post-election crackdown produced **more than 2,000 arrests** (at least
  **2,400 arbitrary arrests** by some counts), including minors and activists,
  on accusations of plotting to overthrow the government.
- Members of **Machado's campaign staff** were arrested; opposition members who
  took refuge in the **Argentine embassy** were denied safe passage out of the
  country.
- Detainees were charged in **virtual hearings** with vague offenses —
  **"incitement to hatred," "terrorism," "conspiracy"** — without regard for due
  process.
- **As of 2026**, opposition activists remain **in hiding or in exile**. Reporting
  in the first half of 2026 describes an atmosphere in which, "just as under
  Maduro, there is almost no room for dissent," and quotes an activist's family
  member: *"We should not have to be scared of expressing ourselves."*
- Machado has publicly characterized the interim government's concessions as
  taken **not voluntarily but in response to U.S. pressure** — directly relevant
  to whether the change is durable.

**Rank-and-file exposure.** Note for the declaration and the brief that the
documented target class is not limited to national figures. It includes campaign
volunteers, poll witnesses (*testigos electorales*), signature collectors,
neighborhood organizers, students, and people who merely posted online or
celebrated in the street. If your client's profile is modest, § 5 (27 people
detained for celebrating; 15 of them children) is the fact to foreground — it
establishes that *no* threshold of prominence is required to be detained.

## 9. Civic space, press, and the absence of state protection

**Journalists.** HRW's World Report 2026 reports **at least 15 journalists
behind bars** as of August 5 (2025), and **167 documented violations of freedom
of expression** between January and August recorded by **Espacio Público**,
including censorship, intimidation, and administrative restrictions. Fourteen
journalists were detained on January 5, 2026 alone. U.S. foreign aid cuts have
undermined independent outlets, several of which are struggling to survive —
shrinking the documentary record going forward.

**NGOs and human rights defenders.** The **"anti-NGO law"** (Law for the
Control, Regularization, Operations and Financing of Non-Governmental and
Related Organizations, passed August 2024) remains in force. Amnesty
characterized it as jeopardizing the existence of humanitarian and human rights
organizations through ambiguous provisions permitting harsh penalties and mass
arbitrary de-registration, and described a **"systematic policy of repression
against civil society."** Families of human rights defenders face harassment,
violence, and enforced disappearance.

**Colectivos.** Armed pro-government civilian groups (*colectivos*) have been a
focus of FFM investigation; protesters and bystanders have died at the hands of
security forces **and colectivos** acting in concert with them. Their continued
operation is significant for two reasons: it establishes a **non-state persecutor
the government is unwilling to control**, and it forecloses the argument that
avoiding formal state contact would keep the applicant safe.

**Aggregate civic-space rating.** Freedom House rates Venezuela **Not Free**,
with an aggregate **Freedom in the World 2026 score of 13 out of 100**. Useful as
a single-figure summary exhibit: the score reflects conditions through the end of
2025 and had not improved on the strength of the transition.

**Judiciary — no meaningful state protection.** HRW's World Report 2026 states
the judiciary **ceased functioning as an independent branch in 2004**, when
Chávez packed the Supreme Court; it has since supported the executive in
repressing critics. Documented due process violations in 2024–2025 include
charging people with serious crimes **without evidence**, **withholding
detainees' whereabouts** from families and counsel, and **preventing detainees
from choosing their own lawyers**. OHCHR has reported government "delays" in
investigating and prosecuting protest-related killings from 2014, 2017, and 2019
and killings by security forces. The same judiciary now administers the amnesty
(§ 6).

> **For the internal relocation analysis** (8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(3)): the
> persecutor is the national government, acting through national intelligence
> and military-counterintelligence services and a nationally deployed National
> Guard, under a national state of emergency, with a captured national
> judiciary and armed irregular auxiliaries operating nationwide. There is no
> region of Venezuela outside the reach of these actors. Relocation is neither
> reasonable nor available.

## 10. Returnees and deportees — critical for CAT and for hardship

This section is likely to be the most contested factual terrain in a 2026
Venezuelan case, and it contains the most serious recent development.

**Volume.** Deportation flights resumed under a 2025 U.S.–Venezuela arrangement.
Roughly **23,067** Venezuelans had been returned cumulatively under the program;
**4,096** across 24 flights in 2026 alone; approximately **6,835** returned
between February and July 2026. Returns run at hundreds per week.

**Government handling of returnees.** Arrivals land at **Simón Bolívar
International Airport (Maiquetía), La Guaira**, and are processed under the
state-run *Gran Misión Vuelta a la Patria*. The government presents this as a
"comprehensive social care protocol" — medical screening, psychological
counseling, legal assistance. Independent reporting describes a different
reality: returnees **compelled to sign documents barring them from disclosing
information about their arrest or legal proceedings**, and in some cases
**compelled to record videos stating their rights were respected**. Coerced
silence on arrival is not reintegration support; it is evidence of what the
state is concealing.

**The June 24, 2026 deportee deaths.** A group of **147 Venezuelans deported
from Texas** landed at Maiquetía aboard Flight 164. From the airport they were
transported **by SEBIN agents** to the Hotel Santuario La Llanada, a remote
mountainous site in La Guaira, where they were confined. Hours later the M7.2
and M7.5 earthquakes destroyed the building. **Only 12 of the 147 survived.**
Reporting indicates SEBIN agents on site **refused to unlock the doors** as the
structure collapsed, leaving returnees trapped.

> **Handle this fact with care and verify it before you plead it.** The
> refusal-to-unlock allegation traces to lower-reliability outlets; the
> underlying event (the flight, the SEBIN transport, the confinement, the death
> toll) is corroborated by CNN, NPR, and Wikipedia's earthquake coverage. Plead
> what CNN and NPR support; treat the locked-doors allegation as requiring
> independent confirmation. Even on the conservative version, the fact that
> deportees were **taken into state custody by the intelligence service and
> confined in a government facility rather than released to their families**
> is highly probative that returnees are treated as a security population, not
> as repatriated citizens.

**Historical returnee mistreatment.** In March 2025, **238 Venezuelans were
deported to El Salvador's CECOT** maximum-security prison without trial,
sentence, or due process, in defiance of a federal court order; over 250 were
later returned to Venezuela under a July 2025 arrangement, with others still
detained or unaccounted for. Nearly 200 Venezuelans were also flown home from
Guantánamo Bay.

**TPS context.** DHS determined Venezuela no longer met TPS conditions; the
Supreme Court's **October 3, 2025** emergency-docket order allowed the
terminations to take effect pending merits litigation, notwithstanding a court
of appeals ruling that the termination was unlawful. The 2021 designation was
separately terminated effective **November 7, 2025**. A subset of beneficiaries
retain work authorization through **October 2, 2026**. After Maduro's capture,
DHS confirmed Venezuelans would **not** receive new TPS protection and that
deportations would continue.

**USCIS adjudication holds and the *Dorcas* vacatur — check your client's
posture.** By policy memorandum **PM-602-0192 (December 2, 2025)**, USCIS
suspended adjudication of benefit applications for nationals of designated
"high-risk" countries including Venezuela, and directed personnel to suspend
processing of **all Form I-589 applications regardless of nationality** (the
"Global Asylum Hold"); **PM-602-0194 (January 1, 2026)** extended the approach
to additional countries. USCIS announced a partial lift of the asylum hold on
**March 30, 2026**.

On **June 5, 2026**, in ***Dorcas International Institute of Rhode Island v.
USCIS***, Chief Judge John J. McConnell Jr. (D.R.I.) held four of these policies
unlawful under the APA and in excess of statutory authority, and **vacated them
nationwide**. USCIS stated it disagrees but would comply and discontinue
enforcement. The government appealed to the **First Circuit**; as of this
research **no stay had issued and the nationwide vacatur remains in place**.

Practical effect: affirmative I-589 adjudication for Venezuelan nationals should
be proceeding. If a client's case is still frozen, *Dorcas* is the citation.
Confirm the appeal's status before relying on this.

**U.S. government's own position on conditions.** The State Department maintains
Venezuela at **Level 4 — Do Not Travel**, updated January 2026, citing
**wrongful detention, torture in detention**, terrorism, kidnapping, arbitrary
enforcement of local laws, crime, civil unrest, and collapsed health
infrastructure, and describing a **"very high risk" of wrongful detention**. All
U.S. consular services in Venezuela — routine and emergency — **remain
suspended**, and the U.S. government states it is **unable to provide emergency
services** to U.S. citizens there.

> That last point deserves emphasis in any brief. The United States takes the
> position that it cannot protect its **own citizens** in Venezuela and that
> they face torture in detention there. That position is irreconcilable with an
> argument that a Venezuelan asylum applicant with a documented political
> profile can safely be returned.

## 11. Humanitarian and security conditions

- **Earthquakes, June 24, 2026.** Two events (M7.2, M7.5) 39 seconds apart
  devastated Caracas, La Guaira, and northern states. **>5,500 confirmed dead,
  ~16,700 injured, tens of thousands missing**; approximately **80% of buildings
  in La Guaira collapsed.** Nationwide state of emergency declared. Venezuela
  secured $346 million in IMF emergency financing.
- **Recovery obstructed.** Civil society warned that widespread poverty,
  sanctions, government control of information, and **state hostility toward
  humanitarian organizations** would impede recovery — the anti-NGO law
  operating directly against earthquake response capacity.
- **Displacement.** Nearly **7.9 million** Venezuelan refugees and migrants
  globally; **6.2 million** hosted in Latin America and the Caribbean as of June
  2026 — one of the largest displacement crises in the world, and itself
  evidence that conditions have not normalized.
- **Baseline collapse.** Persistent shortages of food, medicine, and essential
  services; hyperinflation; violent crime; and collapsed health infrastructure
  predate and compound the earthquake damage.

The earthquakes matter to the legal argument in a specific way: they have
displaced Venezuela's human rights situation from the international agenda
(reporting notes Venezuela's abuses now "struggle for attention"), reduced
monitoring capacity, and given the state an emergency framework within which to
operate — while the U.S. continues returning people into it.

## 12. Accountability and impunity

- **ICC Venezuela I** — Pre-Trial Chamber I authorized the Prosecutor to resume
  the investigation on **June 27, 2023**; it concerns crimes against humanity in
  crackdowns on protesters since 2014, and remains ongoing.
- **ICC withdrawal.** On **July 24–25, 2026**, Venezuela notified the UN of its
  "firm and irrevocable decision" to withdraw from the Rome Statute, alleging
  geographic bias. The ICC confirmed withdrawal **does not affect cooperation
  obligations** arising from investigations already commenced.
- **Universal jurisdiction.** HRW published guidance on universal jurisdiction as
  a route to justice for Venezuelan crimes (June 2, 2026).

**Argue the withdrawal as consciousness of guilt and as evidence against
durability.** A government genuinely transitioning toward accountability does not
exit the tribunal investigating its security forces six months into the
transition. The withdrawal, in July 2026, is the single clearest signal that the
interim government intends to preserve impunity for the apparatus described in
§ 4 — and it post-dates every positive development DHS will cite.

### Caution — the State Department reports have been scaled back

Relevant both to what you cite and to what DHS may cite against you.

**Timing.** The reports are due by statute on **February 25** each year. In
practice they slip to March or April. The 2024 edition slipped to **August 12,
2025**. As of July 27, 2026 the 2025 edition is roughly **five months past the
statutory deadline** and has not issued; if it follows last year's pattern it may
land in August 2026. Do not build a filing schedule around it.

**Reduced scope.** Following the 2025 reorganization of the State Department —
which closed most offices of the Bureau of Democracy, Human Rights, and Labor,
including most DRL regional offices, and dismissed roughly 60 contractors — the
reports were **pared back toward the statutory minimum**. Reporting indicates
drafters were instructed to remove sections on arbitrary or unlawful interference
with privacy, extensive gender-based violence, involuntary medical or
psychological practices, restrictions on internet freedom, and violence or
discrimination against LGBTQ people and people with disabilities. Human Rights
Watch's assessment of the 2024 edition was that it mixed "facts, deception, and
political spin"; CNN reported it omitted detail on abuses in politically allied
countries.

**Two consequences for your case.**

1. **Do not treat a thin 2025 report as evidence of improved conditions.** If the
   Venezuela chapter is shorter or drops categories that appeared in 2023 and
   2024, that reflects a change in U.S. reporting policy, not a change in
   Venezuela. Be ready to show the year-over-year structural comparison — the
   2023 and 2024 PDFs are in the source list (#20, #51) for exactly this.
2. **The omission argument cuts both ways, so make it first.** If DHS points to
   the absence of a category in the current report, the answer is that the
   category was removed department-wide across nearly 200 countries. Sources #52
   and #53 document that.

The corollary: **Tier 1 weight in a 2026 Venezuelan case sits with the UN
Fact-Finding Mission, HRW, Amnesty International, and Foro Penal, not with the
State Department report.** Cite the State Department for the travel advisory and
the consular-services suspension (§ 10), which are current, official, and adverse
to the government's own removal posture.

## 14. Practice notes

**Lead with continuity, not with atrocity.** The 2014–2025 record is not in
dispute and the adjudicator likely knows it. The contested question is
*durability*. Structure the country conditions section around § 4 (apparatus
intact) and § 5 (new detentions post-transition), and use the historical record
as support rather than as the centerpiece.

**Date-anchor everything.** Every fact DHS cites for changed conditions comes
from January–February 2026. Nearly every fact in §§ 4–7 and § 12 post-dates it:
the FFM's March 12 update, HRW's May 13 amnesty analysis, Foro Penal's June 9
count, Amnesty's April urgent action, the June 24 deportee deaths, and the
July 24 ICC withdrawal. Build a two-column chronology exhibit — "what changed"
against "what happened after" — and let the second column run longer.

**Plead CAT in the alternative and develop it fully.** Where a changed-
circumstances argument threatens the asylum claim, CAT is the more durable
protection: no nexus requirement, no discretionary denial, mandatory if the
standard is met. Sections 7 and 10 are built for it.

**Preserve the past-persecution presumption.** If past persecution is
established, the burden is on DHS to show a fundamental change by a
preponderance — 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(ii). Make DHS carry it. Its evidence
is a six-month-old amnesty administered by the same courts, with exclusions
tracking protected speech, leaving convictions intact and hundreds still
detained. Consider also humanitarian asylum under
§ 1208.13(b)(1)(iii)(A) for severe past persecution, and
§ 1208.13(b)(1)(iii)(B) for other serious harm on return — the earthquake
devastation, collapsed health infrastructure, and treatment of returnees in
§§ 10–11 support the latter independent of political nexus.

**Do not overstate the transition's fragility, and do not understate it.** The
July 15, 2026 government–opposition talks are real, and the adjudicator may
raise them. The accurate characterization: a negotiation process has begun,
Machado and González participate cautiously, the U.S. State Department itself
says elections must await security, economic, and press-freedom conditions that
do not yet exist, and no election has been scheduled. A prospective and
unscheduled political process is not a present change in circumstances.
